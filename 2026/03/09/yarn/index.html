Transformers have no built-in notion of order. Feed the same set of tokens in any permutation and the attention scores are unchanged — making two sequences identical from the model’s perspective even if their meanings are entirely different. Positional encoding patches this gap: it injects sequence position into each token’s representation so that the model can tell “the cat sat on the mat” apart from “the mat sat on the cat.”

A brief aside: in a causal (decoder-only) transformer, the autoregressive mask already breaks strict permutation symmetry — a token at position mmm can attend to exactly the mmm tokens before it, so the size of its visible context grows monotonically with its index. This gives the model an implicit sense of “depth” into the sequence. The causal mask reveals how many predecessors each token has, however, not how far apart any two tokens are — a distinction that positional encoding captures directly. For encoder models without such a mask, positional encoding carries even more of the burden.

This post traces the evolution of positional encoding from the original sinusoidal scheme through Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) and its long-context extensions — Position Interpolation (PI), NTK-aware scaling, and finally YaRN — deriving each step carefully along the way.

1. Sinusoidal Positional Encoding

Ignoring multi-head structure, the projected query matrix Q∈RS×DH\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times D_H}Q∈RS×DH​ is a row-stack of per-token vectors:

Q=[q0Tq1T⋮qS−1T]\mathbf{Q} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{q}_0^{\mathsf{T}} \\ \mathbf{q}_1^{\mathsf{T}} \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{q}_{S-1}^{\mathsf{T}} \end{bmatrix} Q=​q0T​q1T​⋮qS−1T​​​

where qm∈RDH\mathbf{q}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{D_H}qm​∈RDH​ is the query vector for the token at position mmm. The simplest positional fix is additive: replace qm\mathbf{q}_mqm​ with qm+pm\mathbf{q}_m + \mathbf{p}_mqm​+pm​, where pm\mathbf{p}_mpm​ depends only on mmm.

Frequency vector. Define DH2\frac{D_H}{2}2DH​​ angular frequencies, one per dimension pair jjj:

ωj=1θ2jDH,j=0,1,…,DH2−1\omega_j = \frac{1}{\theta^{2\frac{j}{D_H}}}, \quad j = 0, 1, \ldots, \frac{D_H}{2}-1 ωj​=θ2DH​j​1​,j=0,1,…,2DH​​−1

with base θ=10,000\theta = 10{,}000θ=10,000. The highest frequency is ω0=1\omega_0 = 1ω0​=1 and the frequencies decay geometrically: ωDH2−1=θ−2(DH2−1)DH≈1θ\omega_{\frac{D_H}{2}-1} = \theta^{-\frac{2(\frac{D_H}{2}-1)}{D_H}} \approx \frac{1}{\theta}ω2DH​​−1​=θ−DH​2(2DH​​−1)​≈θ1​.

Position encoding vector. For the token at position mmm:

pm=[sin⁡(mω0),  cos⁡(mω0),  …,  sin⁡(mωDH2−1),  cos⁡(mωDH2−1)]T\mathbf{p}_m = \bigl[\sin(m\omega_0),\;\cos(m\omega_0),\;\ldots,\;\sin(m\omega_{\frac{D_H}{2}-1}),\;\cos(m\omega_{\frac{D_H}{2}-1})\bigr]^{\mathsf{T}} pm​=[sin(mω0​),cos(mω0​),…,sin(mω2DH​​−1​),cos(mω2DH​​−1​)]T

Each position gets a unique fingerprint and nearby positions remain close in encoding space. The main limitation is that the encoding is absolute: the model sees positions mmm and nnn but must infer their relative gap n−mn - mn−m from the encoded values. Rotary encoding resolves this algebraically.

2. Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)

Instead of adding a position vector, RoPE rotates query and key vectors in 2D subspaces before computing attention. The encoding of relative distance is then exact and guaranteed by the structure of the rotation group.

2.1 The Rotation Matrix

For the token at position mmm, define the block-diagonal rotation matrix Rm∈RDH×DH\mathbf{R}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{D_H \times D_H}Rm​∈RDH​×DH​:

Rm=[Rm(0)Rm(1)⋱RmDH2−1]\mathbf{R}_m = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}_m^{(0)} & & & \\ & \mathbf{R}_m^{(1)} & & \\ & & \ddots & \\ & & & \mathbf{R}_m^{\frac{D_H}{2}-1} \end{bmatrix} Rm​=​Rm(0)​​Rm(1)​​⋱​Rm2DH​​−1​​​

where the jjj-th 2×22\times 22×2 block rotates dimension pair (2j, 2j+1)(2j,\,2j+1)(2j,2j+1) by angle mωjm\omega_jmωj​:

Rm(j)=[cos⁡(mωj)−sin⁡(mωj)sin⁡(mωj)cos⁡(mωj)]\mathbf{R}_m^{(j)} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(m\omega_j) & -\sin(m\omega_j) \\ \sin(m\omega_j) & \cos(m\omega_j) \end{bmatrix} Rm(j)​=[cos(mωj​)sin(mωj​)​−sin(mωj​)cos(mωj​)​]

Φtrain=j01⋯(S−1)00⋅θ−0DH1⋅θ−0DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−0DH10⋅θ−2DH1⋅θ−2DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−2DH⋮⋮⋮⋱⋮DH−220⋅θ−2(DH2−1)DH1⋅θ−2(DH2−1)DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−2(DH2−1)DH\def\arraystretch{1.5} \Phi_{\text{train}} = \begin{array}{c|lllll} j & 0 & 1 & \cdots & (S-1) \\\hline 0 & 0 \cdot \theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} & 1 \cdot \theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1) \cdot \theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} \\ 1 & 0 \cdot \theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} & 1 \cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \tfrac{D_H-2}{2} & 0 \cdot \theta^{-\frac{2(\frac{D_H}{2}-1)}{D_H}} & 1 \cdot\theta^{-\frac{2(\frac{D_H}{2}-1)}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2(\frac{D_H}{2}-1)}{D_H}} \end{array} Φtrain​=j01⋮2DH​−2​​00⋅θ−DH​0​0⋅θ−DH​2​⋮0⋅θ−DH​2(2DH​​−1)​​11⋅θ−DH​0​1⋅θ−DH​2​⋮1⋅θ−DH​2(2DH​​−1)​​⋯⋯⋯⋱⋯​(S−1)(S−1)⋅θ−DH​0​(S−1)⋅θ−DH​2​⋮(S−1)⋅θ−DH​2(2DH​​−1)​​​

This grid serves as the central reference for every context-extension method in this post. RoPE applies the rotation matrix to each token’s query and key before attention:

q~m=Rmqm,k~m=Rmkm\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_m = \mathbf{R}_m \mathbf{q}_m, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{k}}_m = \mathbf{R}_m \mathbf{k}_m q~​m​=Rm​qm​,k~m​=Rm​km​

2.2 Relative Distance Through Rotation

After applying RoPE, stack the rotated vectors into matrices:

Q~=[(R0 q0)T(R1 q1)T⋮(RS−1 qS−1)T],K~=[(R0 k0)T(R1 k1)T⋮(RS−1 kS−1)T]\tilde{\mathbf{Q}} = \begin{bmatrix} (\mathbf{R}_0\,\mathbf{q}_0)^{\mathsf{T}} \\ (\mathbf{R}_1\,\mathbf{q}_1)^{\mathsf{T}} \\ \vdots \\ (\mathbf{R}_{S-1}\,\mathbf{q}_{S-1})^{\mathsf{T}} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \tilde{\mathbf{K}} = \begin{bmatrix} (\mathbf{R}_0\,\mathbf{k}_0)^{\mathsf{T}} \\ (\mathbf{R}_1\,\mathbf{k}_1)^{\mathsf{T}} \\ \vdots \\ (\mathbf{R}_{S-1}\,\mathbf{k}_{S-1})^{\mathsf{T}} \end{bmatrix} Q~​=​(R0​q0​)T(R1​q1​)T⋮(RS−1​qS−1​)T​​,K~=​(R0​k0​)T(R1​k1​)T⋮(RS−1​kS−1​)T​​

The unnormalized attention matrix is A=Q~K~T\mathbf{A} = \tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{\mathsf{T}}A=Q~​K~T. Expanding the (m,n)(m,n)(m,n) entry (ignoring the 1DH\frac{1}{\sqrt{D_H}}DH​​1​ scale):

Amn=q~mT k~n=(Rmqm)T(Rnkn)=qmT RmTRn kn\begin{align*} \mathbf{A}_{mn} &= \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_m^{\mathsf{T}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{k}}_n \\ &= (\mathbf{R}_m\mathbf{q}_m)^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbf{R}_n\mathbf{k}_n) \\ &= \mathbf{q}_m^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{R}_m^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_n\,\mathbf{k}_n \end{align*} Amn​​=q~​mT​k~n​=(Rm​qm​)T(Rn​kn​)=qmT​RmT​Rn​kn​​

The key step is the composition law RmTRn=Rn−m\mathbf{R}_m^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_n = \mathbf{R}_{n-m}RmT​Rn​=Rn−m​. Each jjj-th block verifies this directly via the angle-addition identity:

Rm(j) ⁣TRn(j)=[cos⁡(mωj)sin⁡(mωj)−sin⁡(mωj)cos⁡(mωj)][cos⁡(nωj)−sin⁡(nωj)sin⁡(nωj)cos⁡(nωj)]=[cos⁡((n−m)ωj)−sin⁡((n−m)ωj)sin⁡((n−m)ωj)cos⁡((n−m)ωj)]=Rn−m(j)\def\arraystretch{1.5} \begin{align*} {\mathbf{R}_m^{(j)}}^{\!\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_n^{(j)} &= \begin{bmatrix}\cos(m\omega_j) & \sin(m\omega_j) \\ -\sin(m\omega_j) & \cos(m\omega_j)\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\cos(n\omega_j) & -\sin(n\omega_j) \\ \sin(n\omega_j) & \cos(n\omega_j)\end{bmatrix} \\ &= \begin{bmatrix}\cos((n-m)\omega_j) & -\sin((n-m)\omega_j) \\ \sin((n-m)\omega_j) & \cos((n-m)\omega_j)\end{bmatrix} \\ &= \mathbf{R}_{n-m}^{(j)} \end{align*} Rm(j)​TRn(j)​​=​cos(mωj​)−sin(mωj​)​sin(mωj​)cos(mωj​)​​​cos(nωj​)sin(nωj​)​−sin(nωj​)cos(nωj​)​​=​cos((n−m)ωj​)sin((n−m)ωj​)​−sin((n−m)ωj​)cos((n−m)ωj​)​​=Rn−m(j)​​

Therefore:

Amn=qmT Rn−m kn\mathbf{A}_{mn} = \mathbf{q}_m^{\mathsf{T}}\,\mathbf{R}_{n-m}\,\mathbf{k}_n Amn​=qmT​Rn−m​kn​

The absolute positions mmm and nnn cancel entirely; only the relative offset n−mn - mn−m survives. Writing out all S2S^2S2 entries:

A=[q0Tk0q0TR1k1⋯q0TRS−1kS−1q1TR−1k0q1Tk1⋯q1TRS−2kS−1⋮⋮⋱⋮qS−1TR1−Sk0qS−1TR2−Sk1⋯qS−1TkS−1]\def\arraystretch{1.5} \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{q}_0^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{k}_0 & \mathbf{q}_0^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{1} \mathbf{k}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{q}_0^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{S-1} \mathbf{k}_{S-1} \\ \mathbf{q}_1^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{-1} \mathbf{k}_0 & \mathbf{q}_1^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{k}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{q}_1^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{S-2} \mathbf{k}_{S-1} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \mathbf{q}_{S-1}^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{1-S} \mathbf{k}_0 & \mathbf{q}_{S-1}^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{2-S} \mathbf{k}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{q}_{S-1}^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{k}_{S-1} \end{bmatrix} A=​q0T​k0​q1T​R−1​k0​⋮qS−1T​R1−S​k0​​q0T​R1​k1​q1T​k1​⋮qS−1T​R2−S​k1​​⋯⋯⋱⋯​q0T​RS−1​kS−1​q1T​RS−2​kS−1​⋮qS−1T​kS−1​​​

Each entry depends only on the token content vectors and the relative displacement n−mn - mn−m. This is an exact algebraic identity, not an approximation, and generalizes cleanly to any (m,n)(m, n)(m,n) pair — including those encountered only after context extension.

3. Naive Extrapolation and Its Limits

3.1 The Frequency Grid

As established in §2.1, the training frequency grid Φtrain\Phi_{\text{train}}Φtrain​ collects every angle ϕj,m=mωj\phi_{j,m} = m\omega_jϕj,m​=mωj​ seen during training:

Φtrain=j01⋯S−100⋅θ−0DH1⋅θ−0DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−0DH10⋅θ−2DH1⋅θ−2DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−2DH⋮⋮⋮⋱⋮DH−220⋅θ−DH−2DH1⋅θ−DH−2DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−DH−2DH\def\arraystretch{2} \Phi_{\text{train}} = \begin{array}{c|llll} j & 0 & 1 & \cdots & S-1 \\\hline 0 & 0\cdot\theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} & 1\cdot\theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} \\ 1 & 0\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} & 1\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \tfrac{D_H-2}{2} & 0\cdot\theta^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} & 1\cdot\theta^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} \end{array} Φtrain​=j01⋮2DH​−2​​00⋅θ−DH​0​0⋅θ−DH​2​⋮0⋅θ−DH​DH​−2​​11⋅θ−DH​0​1⋅θ−DH​2​⋮1⋅θ−DH​DH​−2​​⋯⋯⋯⋱⋯​S−1(S−1)⋅θ−DH​0​(S−1)⋅θ−DH​2​⋮(S−1)⋅θ−DH​DH​−2​​​

Naive extrapolation simply extends this grid rightward with unchanged frequencies, appending columns for positions SSS through S′−1S'-1S′−1:

Φextrap=j0⋯(S−1)S⋯(S′−1)00⋅θ−0DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−0DHS⋅θ−0DH⋯(S′−1)⋅θ−0DH10⋅θ−2DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−2DHS⋅θ−2DH⋯(S′−1)⋅θ−2DH⋮⋮⋱⋮⋮⋱⋮DH−220⋅θ−DH−2DH⋯(S−1)⋅θ−DH−2DHS⋅θ−DH−2DH⋯(S′−1)⋅θ−DH−2DH\def\arraystretch{2} \Phi_{\text{extrap}} = \begin{array}{c|lll|lll} j & 0 & \cdots & (S-1) & S & \cdots & (S'-1) \\\hline 0 & 0\cdot\theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} & S\cdot\theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S'-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{0}{D_H}} \\ 1 & 0\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} & S\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S'-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{2}{D_H}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \tfrac{D_H-2}{2} & 0\cdot\theta^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} & S\cdot\theta^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} & \cdots & (S'-1)\cdot\theta^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} \end{array} Φextrap​=j01⋮2DH​−2​​00⋅θ−DH​0​0⋅θ−DH​2​⋮0⋅θ−DH​DH​−2​​⋯⋯⋯⋱⋯​(S−1)(S−1)⋅θ−DH​0​(S−1)⋅θ−DH​2​⋮(S−1)⋅θ−DH​DH​−2​​SS⋅θ−DH​0​S⋅θ−DH​2​⋮S⋅θ−DH​DH​−2​​⋯⋯⋯⋱⋯​(S′−1)(S′−1)⋅θ−DH​0​(S′−1)⋅θ−DH​2​⋮(S′−1)⋅θ−DH​DH​−2​​​

3.2 High-Frequency vs. Low-Frequency Dimensions

The wavelength (period for one full 2π2\pi2π rotation) of dimension pair jjj is:

Tj=2πωj=2π θ2jDHT_j = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_j} = 2\pi\,\theta^{\frac{2j}{D_H}} Tj​=ωj​2π​=2πθDH​2j​

Since TjT_jTj​ grows with jjj, there exists a critical dimension jcj_cjc​ at which the wavelength equals the training length SSS. Dimensions below jcj_cjc​ fit at least one full cycle inside the training window; dimensions above do not:

Tjc=S  ⟹  2π θ2jcDH=S  ⟹  jc=DH2log⁡θS2πT_{j_c} = S \;\Longrightarrow\; 2\pi\,\theta^{2\frac{j_c}{D_H}} = S \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{j_c = \frac{D_H}{2}\log_\theta\frac{S}{2\pi}} Tjc​​=S⟹2πθ2DH​jc​​=S⟹jc​=2DH​​logθ​2πS​​

This divides all dimension pairs into two qualitatively different regimes:

  • High-frequency dimensions (j≪jcj \ll j_cj≪jc​, Tj≪ST_j \ll STj​≪S): the angle mωjm\omega_jmωj​ completes at least one full revolution within the training window [0,S−1][0, S-1][0,S−1]. These dimensions have been exposed to the full range of rotation phases [0,2π)[0, 2\pi)[0,2π) during training. Extrapolating to m>Sm > Sm>S merely continues a familiar oscillation, so they degrade gracefully.

  • Low-frequency dimensions (j≫jcj \gg j_cj≫jc​, Tj≫ST_j \gg STj​≫S): the angle mωjm\omega_jmωj​ does not complete even a single revolution during training, reaching only the fraction STj<1\frac{S}{T_j} < 1Tj​S​<1 of the full cycle. These dimensions have seen only a narrow arc of [0,2π)[0, 2\pi)[0,2π). Extending to S′>SS' > SS′>S pushes them into angles they have never encountered at training time (out-of-distribution, OOD), causing the attention distribution to deteriorate and generation quality to sharply degrade.

Naive extrapolation treats both regimes identically — extending every row of Φextrap\Phi_{\text{extrap}}Φextrap​ with the same frequencies — and it is precisely the low-frequency rows (j>jcj > j_cj>jc​) that break down under this treatment.

4. Position Interpolation (PI)

4.1 The Linear Rescaling

Position Interpolation takes the opposite approach: compress the target position range [0,S′−1][0, S'-1][0,S′−1] back into the training range [0,S−1][0, S-1][0,S−1] by scaling every position by k=SS′k = \frac{S}{S'}k=S′S​:

The angle in dimension jjj at position mmm becomes mωj=mkωjm\omega_j = mk\omega_jmωj​=mkωj​. The resulting frequency grid is:

Since (S′−1)k=(S′−1)⋅SS′<S(S'-1)k = (S'-1)\cdot\tfrac{S}{S'} < S(S′−1)k=(S′−1)⋅S′S​<S, every entry stays within the range of Φtrain\Phi_{\text{train}}Φtrain​. No angle ever exceeds the training-time maximum: the OOD problem disappears.

Every row is identical: the entire grid is a constant SS′\frac{S}{S'}S′S​ matrix. This uniformity is both PI’s key property (it stays in-distribution for all jjj) and its principal weakness (it does not respect the very different needs of high- and low-frequency dimensions).

4.2 The Cost: Resolution Loss

Compression solves the OOD problem but introduces a new one. The per-token angle step for dimension jjj is ωj\omega_jωj​ before interpolation and only ωjs\frac{\omega_j}{s}sωj​​ after — a uniform sss-fold reduction across every row of ΦPI\Phi_{\text{PI}}ΦPI​. For high-frequency dimensions this matters acutely: they rely on fine angular granularity to distinguish nearby tokens, and an sss-fold reduction washes out exactly that granularity. For low-frequency dimensions the picture is doubly problematic: the angular step ωj\omega_jωj​ is already tiny (since Tj≫1T_j \gg 1Tj​≫1), so after compression the step ωjs\frac{\omega_j}{s}sωj​​ becomes even smaller — nearby tokens become nearly indistinguishable in these dimensions, erasing what little positional signal they carried.

In short: PI trades OOD degradation for resolution loss, and both problems arise from treating all frequency dimensions identically. Fine-tuning on longer sequences is typically required to recover the high-frequency resolution.

5. NTK-aware Scaling

5.1 The Base-Scaling Idea

NTK-aware RoPE addresses the high/low-frequency asymmetry with a single parameter change: replace the base θ\thetaθ with a scaled base αθ\alpha\thetaαθ, leaving position indices mmm unchanged.

The modified frequency grid makes this per-dimension compression visible at a glance:

The scaling factor relative to Φextrap\Phi_{\text{extrap}}Φextrap​ depends only on the row jjj, not on the position mmm:

ΛNTK(j)=α−2jDH\Lambda_{\text{NTK}}(j) = \alpha^{-\frac{2j}{D_H}} ΛNTK​(j)=α−DH​2j​

5.2 Determining the Scale Factor α\alphaα

The two boundary values of Λj\Lambda_jΛj​ correspond to the two extreme strategies: Λj=1\Lambda_j = 1Λj​=1 means no compression at all — pure extrapolation, as in Φextrap\Phi_{\text{extrap}}Φextrap​; Λj=SS′\Lambda_j = \frac{S}{S'}Λj​=S′S​ means full compression — every position is rescaled into the training range, as in ΦPI\Phi_{\text{PI}}ΦPI​.

PI’s failure in §4.2 suggests a more refined assignment. Because high-frequency dimensions have seen all rotation phases during training, they tolerate extrapolation (Λj≈1\Lambda_j \approx 1Λj​≈1) without going OOD; compressing them unnecessarily only destroys their angular resolution. Conversely, low-frequency dimensions never completed a full rotation in training, so they go OOD immediately under extrapolation; they need full compression (Λj=SS′\Lambda_j = \frac{S}{S'}Λj​=S′S​) to stay in-distribution — and since their angular steps are already small, the additional loss in resolution is comparatively minor.

NTK-aware therefore targets a decreasing compression spectrum: high-frequency dimensions (small jjj) receive little to no compression, while low-frequency dimensions (large jjj) receive full PI-style compression. The constraint that row j=DH−22j = \frac{D_H-2}{2}j=2DH​−2​ achieves exactly full compression determines α\alphaα:

α−DH−2DH=SS′  ⟹  α=(S′S)DHDH−2\alpha^{-\frac{D_H-2}{D_H}} = \frac{S}{S'} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\alpha = \left(\frac{S'}{S}\right)^{\frac{D_H}{D_H-2}}} α−DH​DH​−2​=S′S​⟹α=(SS′​)DH​−2DH​​​

5.3 The Compression Spectrum and Its Limitation

Substituting α=(S′S)DHDH−2\alpha = \left(\frac{S'}{S}\right)^{\frac{D_H}{D_H-2}}α=(SS′​)DH​−2DH​​ into ΛNTK\Lambda_{\text{NTK}}ΛNTK​ shows the row factors become

The shaded triangle highlights the fundamental limitation of NTK-aware scaling. For dimensions with j>jcj > j_cj>jc​ — whose wavelength exceeds the training length SSS — the model has never seen a full rotation during training, so any Λ>SS′\Lambda > \frac{S}{S'}Λ>S′S​ amounts to partial extrapolation into OOD angles. Yet NTK-aware assigns these dimensions a compression factor strictly greater than SS′\frac{S}{S'}S′S​ (i.e. less compression than PI), because its single exponential law ΛNTK(j)=(SS′)2jDH−2\Lambda_{\text{NTK}}(j) = \left(\frac{S}{S'}\right)^{2\frac{j}{D_H-2}}ΛNTK​(j)=(S′S​)2DH​−2j​ cannot reach full PI compression until the very last row j=DH−22j = \frac{D_H-2}{2}j=2DH​−2​. 6. YaRN: NTK-by-Parts

YaRN (Yet Another RoPE extensioN method) takes a different approach from NTK-aware scaling: instead of spreading interpolation pressure across all dimensions via a base change, it explicitly segments dimensions by frequency and applies a per-dimension mix of naive extrapolation and PI.

6.1 A Unified Interpolation Formula

Using the notation of this post, it is convenient to describe YaRN directly at the level of the per-dimension scaling factor. We write the YaRN scaling factor as a linear interpolation between naive extrapolation (Λ=1\Lambda = 1Λ=1) and PI (Λ=SS′\Lambda = \frac{S}{S'}Λ=S′S​):

ΛYaRN(j)=(1−γj)⋅1  +  γj⋅SS′=1−γj ⁣(1−SS′)\Lambda_{\text{YaRN}}(j) = (1 - \gamma_j) \cdot 1 \;+\; \gamma_j \cdot \frac{S}{S'} = 1 - \gamma_j\!\left(1 - \frac{S}{S'}\right) ΛYaRN​(j)=(1−γj​)⋅1+γj​⋅S′S​=1−γj​(1−S′S​)

where γj∈[0,1]\gamma_j \in [0,1]γj​∈[0,1] controls how strongly dimension jjj is pulled away from naive extrapolation toward full PI-style compression.

This form is useful because the two limiting cases recover the extreme strategies immediately:

ΛYaRN(j)  →  γj ≡ 0    1=Λextrap(j)(Naive extrapolation),ΛYaRN(j)  →  γj ≡ 1    SS′=ΛPI(j)(Position Interpolation).\begin{align*} &\Lambda_{\text{YaRN}}(j) \;\xrightarrow{\;\gamma_j\,\equiv\,0\;}\; 1 = \Lambda_{\text{extrap}}(j) \quad\text{(Naive extrapolation)}, \\ &\Lambda_{\text{YaRN}}(j) \;\xrightarrow{\;\gamma_j\,\equiv\,1\;}\; \frac{S}{S'} = \Lambda_{\text{PI}}(j) \quad\text{(Position Interpolation)}. \end{align*} ​ΛYaRN​(j)γj​≡0​1=Λextrap​(j)(Naive extrapolation),ΛYaRN​(j)γj​≡1​S′S​=ΛPI​(j)(Position Interpolation).​

Thus YaRN can be read as a frequency-dependent bridge between the two extremes: in dimensions where extrapolation is safe (because training has already covered every rotation phase), we set γj\gamma_jγj​ close to 000; in dimensions where full PI-style compression is needed (to avoid OOD angles), we set γj\gamma_jγj​ close to 111.

The remaining question is therefore how γj\gamma_jγj​ should vary with jjj. YaRN answers this with a ramp function over frequency bands. Writing the transition directly in terms of the dimension index, we define

γj={0j<jhj−jhjl−jhjh≤j≤jl1j>jl\gamma_j = \begin{cases} 0 & j < j_h \\[4pt] \dfrac{j - j_h}{j_l - j_h} & j_h \le j \le j_l \\[4pt] 1 & j > j_l \end{cases} γj​=⎩⎨⎧​0jl​−jh​j−jh​​1​j<jh​jh​≤j≤jl​j>jl​​

where jhj_hjh​ and jlj_ljl​ are the high- and low-frequency boundaries derived in §6.2. This gives three regimes immediately:

  • High-frequency band (j<jhj < j_hj<jh​): γj=0\gamma_j = 0γj​=0, so YaRN leaves the frequency untouched (naive extrapolation, Λ=1\Lambda = 1Λ=1).
  • Middle-frequency band (jh≤j≤jlj_h \le j \le j_ljh​≤j≤jl​): γj\gamma_jγj​ increases linearly from 000 to 111, smoothly blending from extrapolation toward PI.
  • Low-frequency band (j>jlj > j_lj>jl​): γj=1\gamma_j = 1γj​=1, so YaRN becomes full PI (Λ=SS′\Lambda = \frac{S}{S'}Λ=S′S​).

Compared with NTK-aware scaling, which commits to one exponential law across all dimensions, YaRN makes the intended inductive bias explicit: high-frequency rows are left completely untouched to preserve local resolution, low-frequency rows are pushed all the way to PI to avoid OOD angles, and only the ambiguous middle region is blended.

6.2 NTK-by-Parts: Frequency Segmentation

The wavelength of dimension pair jjj is:

Tj=2πωj=2π θ2jDHT_j = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_j} = 2\pi\,\theta^{\frac{2j}{D_H}} Tj​=ωj​2π​=2πθDH​2j​

To determine where each regime begins, YaRN measures how many full rotations dimension jjj completes over the original training window. Since STj\frac{S}{T_j}Tj​S​ is exactly the number of rotations completed within [0,S−1][0,S-1][0,S−1], the rule from §6.1 can be restated operationally as follows:

  • many rotations inside training ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ the model has seen all rotation phases, so extrapolation is safe and γj\gamma_jγj​ should stay near 000;
  • fewer than one rotation inside training ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ full PI-style compression is safer, so γj\gamma_jγj​ should move to 111;
  • anything in between receives a smooth ramp from 000 to 111.

YaRN implements this idea with two empirically chosen thresholds.

High-frequency boundary jhj_hjh​. Dimensions completing more than 32 full rotations, i.e., STj>32\frac{S}{T_j} > 32Tj​S​>32:

Tjh=S32  ⟹  2π θ2jhDH=S32  ⟹  jh=DH2log⁡θ ⁣S64πT_{j_h} = \frac{S}{32} \;\Longrightarrow\; 2\pi\,\theta^{2\frac{j_h}{D_H}} = \frac{S}{32} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{j_h = \frac{D_H}{2}\log_\theta\!\frac{S}{64\pi}} Tjh​​=32S​⟹2πθ2DH​jh​​=32S​⟹jh​=2DH​​logθ​64πS​​

Low-frequency boundary jlj_ljl​. Dimensions that do not complete even one full rotation, i.e., STj<1\frac{S}{T_j} < 1Tj​S​<1:

Tjl=S  ⟹  2π θ2jlDH=S  ⟹  jl=DH2log⁡θ ⁣S2πT_{j_l} = S \;\Longrightarrow\; 2\pi\,\theta^{2\frac{j_l}{D_H}} = S \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{j_l = \frac{D_H}{2}\log_\theta\!\frac{S}{2\pi}} Tjl​​=S⟹2πθ2DH​jl​​=S⟹jl​=2DH​​logθ​2πS​​

Thus the three bands are:

  • j<jhj < j_hj<jh​: high frequency, pure extrapolation (no modification);
  • jh≤j≤jlj_h \le j \le j_ljh​≤j≤jl​: middle frequency, linearly ramped interpolation between extrapolation and PI;
  • j>jlj > j_lj>jl​: low frequency, full PI regime.

6.3 Temperature Compensation

Applying any form of position scaling subtly distorts the attention distribution. Compressed position differences m−ns\frac{m - n}{s}sm−n​ reduce the rotation angle entering Rn−m\mathbf{R}_{n-m}Rn−m​, which shrinks the expected variance of the inner product qmTRn−mkn\mathbf{q}_m^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{n-m} \mathbf{k}_nqmT​Rn−m​kn​. A lower variance yields a flatter softmax distribution — equivalent to raising the softmax temperature — which increases perplexity.

YaRN compensates by multiplying the pre-softmax score by a constant t>1t > 1t>1:

Amn=t⋅qmTRn−m kn\mathbf{A}_{mn} = t \cdot \mathbf{q}_m^{\mathsf{T}} \mathbf{R}_{n-m}\, \mathbf{k}_n Amn​=t⋅qmT​Rn−m​kn​

This lowers the effective softmax temperature, sharpening the attention distribution back toward its training-time shape. The YaRN paper reports the calibration t≈0.1ln⁡s+1t \approx 0.1\ln s + 1t≈0.1lns+1.
